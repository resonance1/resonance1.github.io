Evaluating the Political Sustainability of High-Skilled Immigration: Do Local Affordability Shocks Prompt Electoral Backlash?

Jeremy Ferwerda, Dartmouth College

March 16, 2026

Motivation

  • Research suggests citizens strongly prefer high-skilled immigrants to low-skilled immigrants
    • Hainmueller & Hiscox 2010; Helbling & Kriesi 2014; Hainmueller & Hopkins 2015
  • This preference is stable across countries and time periods
    • Naumann et al. 2018; Valentino et al. 2019; Avina et al. 2026
  • Nevertheless, OECD countries differ substantially in the share of permanent economic admissions: e.g. Canada (~58%) vs the United States (~13%)

Motivation

  • Countries that prioritize high-skilled admissions often have points systems, which use competitive entry thresholds based on education, skills, language competency, etc.

Motivation

  • Points systems allow policymakers to flexibly adjust admissions criteria to target specific occupations, skillsets, etc.

Motivation

  • Points systems allow policymakers to flexibly adjust admissions criteria to target specific occupations, skillsets, etc.

  • Many systems also build in administrative discretion to raise or lower admissions targets on a rolling basis to dynamically adjust to the state of the economy.

  • Research suggests that citizens who learn/know about points systems have higher support for immigration, likely because these programs are consistent with economic sociotropic reasoning
    • Farrell et al 2012; Donnelly 2017; Ellerman 2021
  • As a result, scholars have argued that countries with points-based systems can politically sustain higher levels of immigration without incurring voter backlash
    • Triadafilopoulos 2021; Helbling, Maxwell & Traunmüller 2024; Kustov 2025

Motivation

  • Beyond being aligned with public opinion, high-skilled immigration systems may also have positive spillovers that further increase public support.

  • For instance, research suggests that high-skilled immigration:

    • Spurs economic growth (Kerr & Lincoln 2010; Peri 2012)
    • Provides positive fiscal contributions (Dustmann & Frattini 2014; National Academies 2017)
  • Nevertheless, high-skilled immigration may also:
    • Lead to perceptions of overcrowding (Card, Dustmann & Preston 2012)
    • Increase congestion of particular services, e.g. healthcare (Giuntella et al. 2018; Speciale 2012)
    • Prompt backlash to rising ethnic diversity

A Potential Weakness: Affordability

  • In the short-run, high-skilled immigration may have negative spillovers on local affordability.
  • Specifically, high-skilled immigration is likely to have a larger housing market impact than low-skilled immigration:
    • High-skilled immigrants tend to concentrate in particular urban centres — those with high-paying knowledge-economy jobs (Peri et al. 2015; Kerr & Lincoln 2010)
    • They arrive with higher incomes and purchasing power, directly competing with natives for the same housing stock (Gonzalez & Ortega 2013; Sharpe 2019)
    • If construction does not keep pace with demand, price pressure is amplified — and supply is slow to adjust in the short run within dense urban environments (Glaeser & Gyourko 2018; Baum-Snow & Han 2024)
  • This makes shelter costs a likely channel through which voters may experience tangible, short-term costs of high-skilled immigration — even when the long-run effects are positive.

Research Question

  • Focus on the housing channel:
    • How much does a surge in high-skilled immigration impact local housing prices (rents)?
    • How do changes in local rents affect support for pro-/anti- immigrant parties?
  • These topics remain relatively underexplored in the literature:
    • Few studies causally isolate the effect of high-skilled admissions on housing prices.
    • We know that unaffordability polarizes renters and homeowners politically (Ansell & Cansunar 2021), and can lead to increased voting for far-right parties (Abou-Chadi, Cohen & Kurer 2024).
    • But these effects are typically viewed in isolation; not directly linked to the effect of increased immigration.

Empirical Setting: Canada

  • One of the highest shares of economic migrants in the OECD (58% in 2024)
  • High rate of immigration (admits >1% of annual population per year)
  • Persistently high support for immigration; ‘hard case’ for reversal

  • Implemented in January 2015
  • Electronic application management system for economic immigration
  • Candidates ranked by Comprehensive Ranking System (CRS) score; top-scoring admitted every ~2 weeks using arbitrary batch sizes/cutoffs selected by the government
  • Invitees granted permanent residence upon arrival

Housing and Politics in Canada

Context: Housing

  • Express Entry rollout was followed by a sustained increase in rental prices

Policy Responses

  • Provincial rent freezes for existing tenants:
    • In 2017, Ontario expanded rent control to additional units (Rental Fairness Act), extending coverage from units built before 1991 to all units occupied before November 2018
    • In 2018, British Columbia changed allowable annual rent increases from CPI + 2% to CPI only.
  • Foreign investment ban:
    • Prohibition on the Purchase of Residential Property by Non-Canadians Act (Bill C-19). In force January 2023; extended to January 2027. Bars non-permanent residents from purchasing residential property in metropolitan regions.

Rhetoric in Parliament

  • Parties started to causally link immigration to the housing crisis
  • Method: Keyword co-occurrence of all House of Commons speeches
    • Define immigration and housing term dictionaries
    • Classify which speeches mention both immigration + housing
    • Validate causal link with LLMs

Immigration Attitudes

  • In 2023, a majority of Canadians said the government was admitting “too many immigrants” for the first time since 1998 (Environics Focus Canada)
  • By 2024, 58% held this view — the most rapid shift since tracking began in 1977

Immigration Target Cut

  • In October 2024, Liberal government announced a revised 2025–2027 levels plan
    • Cut permanent resident targets from 500K → 395K (2025), declining to 365K (2027)
    • Migration Minister: “Uncontrolled migration is unsustainable”; described the system as “overheated” and in need of a “discipline infusion”
    • Linked changes directly to housing affordability — projected the plan would reduce the housing supply gap by ~670K units by end of 2027

Electoral Campaigns (2025)

    • 2021 (Trudeau): Ran on “Forward. For Everyone” — celebrated diversity, pledged record-high immigration (401K–421K/year), influenced by Century Initiative goal of 100M Canadians by 2100
    • 2025 (Carney): Ran on getting immigration “under control” — stabilize PR admissions below 1% of population
      • Complete reversal in framing: from immigration as unqualified good → immigration requiring limits matched to housing and service capacity

Design

Research Question

How did the increase in high-skilled migration impact local housing markets/political responses?

Data

  • Admissions:
    • Administrative records of all permanent resident admissions by citizenship, admissions category, and landing destination (Census Subdivision)
  • Housing:
    • Canada Mortgage and Housing Corporation conducts the Rental Market Survey annually
    • Enumerators contact landlords/property managers directly — not self-reported by tenants
    • Covers purpose-built rental structures in all urban areas
    • Collects actual rents paid, vacancy rates, and unit characteristics
    • Available at census subdivision and census tract levels
  • Elections:
    • Polling station-level data, mapped with shapefiles to other geographies

Design

How did the increase in high-skilled migration impact local housing markets/political responses?

  • Endogeneity concern: Immigrants are drawn to economically vibrant areas — the same places where rents are already rising and where political attitudes may already be shifting. A naive correlation between immigration and rents (or politics) confounds the causal effect with selection.
  • We need variation in immigration that is exogenous to these patterns.
  • Utilize a shift-share approach with plausibly exogenous variation in the immigration shifts.

Shift-Share

  • Based on the intuition that immigrants are likely to preferentially settle in regions with co-ethnics.
  • Combines national immigration “shifts” with pre-existing settlement “shares”:

\[Z_{j,t} = \sum_o S_{j,o} \cdot A_{o,t}\]

  • \(S_{j,o}\): baseline share of immigrants from origin \(o\) in region \(j\) (from Census) — the “shares”
  • \(A_{o,t}\): national-level PR admissions from origin \(o\) in year \(t\) — the “shifts”

Shift-Share

Shift-share instruments require that either the initial shares or the national-level shifts are exogenous. Express Entry meets the 2nd criterion:

  • Federal selection: the government sets batch sizes and varies CRS score thresholds every 2 weeks — these decisions are made administratively at the national level, not in response to local housing or labor market conditions

  • Nationality-blind: candidates ranked purely on age, education, language, work experience — no origin-country quotas or points

  • Nationality composition of arrivals shifts over time as applicant pools change globally (e.g., surge in Indian applicants post-2015)

Shift-Share

  • One complication is the Provincial Nominee Program (PNP) - provinces may jump the queue and select applicants from the Express Entry pool. If applicants accept the offer, they are admitted, but must settle in that province.
  • Thankfully, this selection procedure is nationality blind
  • So we can decompose admissions: For the PNP component: use within-province nationality shares and province-level PNP admissions shifts
  • For remaining PR streams: retain national-level shares and non-PNP shifts

\[Z^{pnp}_{j,t} = \sum_o S^{prov}_{j,o} \cdot A^{pnp,prov}_{o,t} + \sum_o S^{nat}_{j,o} \cdot A^{nonpnp}_{o,t}\]

Immigration Shocks

For each region, define the cumulative unexpected immigration stock:

\[\Delta I^{pc}_{j} = \sum_{t=2016}^{T} \left( I^{pc}_{j,t} - \bar{I}^{pc}_{j} \right)\]

where \(\bar{I}^{pc}_{j}\) is the pre-Express Entry annual rate of increase.

  • Cumulative formulation captures the total additional population a region has absorbed (per capita) above its pre-Express Entry growth rate.
  • This is a conservative approach; assumes that there is no reaction unless there is a change in slope.

Consider a Census Subdivision with 30,000 residents that received immigrants at a rate of 0.5% of population per year before Express Entry.

Adjusting for Pre-trends

  • We have plausible exogeneity in shifts, but high-exposure regions may have been on different outcome trajectories before 2015
  • IV corrects for endogeneity of actual flows but does not address confounding pre-trends
  • Use the Synthetic IV (SIV) approach (Gulek & Vives i Bastida 2025):
    1. For each region \(j\), find donor weights \(w_{j,k} \geq 0\) that match \(j\)’s pre-treatment outcome trajectory from among CSDs in the same province.
    2. Debias all variables — outcome, treatment, and instrument — by subtracting the synthetic control counterpart.

Evidence 2: Heterogeneity

Evidence 3: Within-CSD Variation

  • Census tracts nested within CSDs allow us to test whether neighborhoods with larger rent increases had larger political responses, holding the immigration shock constant.

Evidence 4: Additional Hansard Analysis

  • Lastly, we can code which speakers in Parliament were likely to link immigration to the housing crisis, and identify their exposure to the (population-weighted) immigration and rent shocks in their constituency.
  • Outcome: Count of an MP’s speeches causally linking immigration to the housing crisis
  • Treatment: Unexpected immigration shock (pp)
  • Sample: 315 MPs, 44th Parliament (2021–2025)

Zero-inflated Poisson model : A 1 SD increase in immigration shock exposure (0.62 pp) increases the rate of immigration-housing speeches by 41% (p < 0.01).

Benchmark: A 1 SD increase in the rental shock (11.9 pp) increases the rate of immigration-housing speeches by 14% (p < 0.05).

Conclusion

  • Immigration shocks causally increased short-run rents (~10% per 1 pp shock) and prompted a significant 2025 electoral backlash (~+2.8 pp Conservative, -3.9 pp Liberal per 1 pp shock)
  • The backlash appears weakly mediated by local rents: emerged only in 2025, uniform across labor market conditions, and not amplified in high-rent neighborhoods

  • Better characterized as a national salience effect: immigration became politically salient post-COVID, and areas with higher exposure were more receptive to anti-immigration messaging

  • Consistent with Hopkins’s (2010) “politicized places” model

Future Steps

  • Model/exploit COVID shock more directly in the analysis; perhaps using rent freezes as another instrument

  • Directly model domestic outmigration/sorting in response to rising rents.

  • Look at impact of shocks on supply – where was housing able to rapidly scale up to meet demand? Did this vary by prior politics? Prior adjustment to shocks?

Other Implications

  • Concentrated immigration to urban areas likely to remain a consistent challenge to the sustainability of high-skilled immigration systems
  • I am co-PI of a project with the Immigration Policy Lab (IPL) where we are working directly with the Canadian government to address this issue.
  • This fall, we began fielding surveys to all incoming Express Entry migrants querying their destination preferences.
  • We see strong + consistent preferences for urban areas with public transport.
  • Nevertheless, migrants value their potential earnings to living costs ratio the highest.

  • We are developing a machine learning algorithm that can suggest potential alternative landing locations that would maximize individuals’ expected disposable income while being consistent with preferences.

  • More broadly, Canada is considering expanding immigration regionalization (like the PNP program) to better distribute immigrants nationally.

Formulas

First stage:

\[\Delta I^{pc}_{j} = \alpha + \beta \cdot \text{Exposure}_j + X'_j \pi + \varepsilon_j\]

Second stage:

\[\Delta \ln \text{Outcome}_j = \alpha + \beta \cdot \Delta \widehat{I^{pc}_j} + X'_j \pi + u_j\]

Bartik Instrument Construction

Predicted immigration inflow to region \(j\) in year \(t\): \[Z_{j,t} = \sum_o S_{j,o} \cdot A_{o,t}\]

where \(S_{j,o}\) = share of immigrants from origin \(o\) in region \(j\) (2016 Census), \(A_{o,t}\) = national PR admissions from origin \(o\) in year \(t\).

Per-capita normalization: \[Z^{pc}_{j,t} = \frac{Z_{j,t}}{\text{Pop}_j}, \qquad I^{pc}_{j,t} = \frac{I_{j,t}}{\text{Pop}_j}\]

Time-averaged exposure (single cross-sectional measure): \[\text{Exp}^{pc}_j = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=2015}^{2024} Z^{pc}_{j,t}\]

Event Study Specifications

First-stage event study (equation 3 in paper): \[I^{pc,\text{shock}}_{j,t} = \alpha_j + \gamma_t + \sum_{t' \neq 2015} \delta_{t'} \, \text{Exposure}_j \cdot \mathbf{1}(t = t') + X'_{j,t}\pi + \varepsilon_{j,t}\]

  • \(I^{pc,\text{shock}}_{j,t} = I^{pc}_{j,t} - I^{pc}_{j,2015}\): deviation of immigration from 2015 baseline
  • \(\delta_{t'}\): how well the instrument predicts immigration in year \(t'\) relative to 2015
  • \(F\)-statistic: joint test that \(\delta_{t'}\) for \(t' \in \{2017,...,2024\}\) are jointly zero

Reduced-form event study (equation 4): \[\log(\text{Rent}_{j,t}) = \alpha_j + \gamma_t + \sum_{t' \neq 2015} \delta_{t'} \, \text{Exposure}_j \cdot \mathbf{1}(t = t') + X'_{j,t}\pi + \varepsilon_{j,t}\]

  • \(\delta_{t'}\): effect of 1 unit more exposure on log rent in year \(t'\), relative to 2015

Cumulative Treatment & Long-Difference

Cumulative unexpected immigration stock: \[\Delta I^{pc,S}_j = \sum_{t=2017}^{2019} \left( I^{pc}_{j,t} - \bar{I}^{pc}_j \right), \qquad \Delta I^{pc,L}_j = \sum_{t=2017}^{2024} \left( I^{pc}_{j,t} - \bar{I}^{pc}_j \right)\]

where \(\bar{I}^{pc}_j = \text{avg } I^{pc}_j\) over 2013–2015 is the baseline annual flow rate.

Long-difference outcomes: \[\Delta \ln \text{Rent}^S_j = \ln \overline{\text{Rent}}^{2017\text{-}19}_j - \ln \overline{\text{Rent}}^{2013\text{-}15}_j\] \[\Delta \ln \text{Rent}^L_j = \ln \overline{\text{Rent}}^{2022\text{-}24}_j - \ln \overline{\text{Rent}}^{2013\text{-}15}_j\]

2SLS instruments \(\Delta I^{pc}_j\) with \(\text{Exposure}_j\) (Indian share, Bartik, or PNP-Bartik). All variables in percentage-point units (\(\times 100\)).

Synthetic Control Debiasing (SIV)

For each region \(j\), find SC weights \(w_{j,k} \geq 0\), \(\sum_k w_{j,k} = 1\) minimizing: \[\left\| \tilde{Y}^{\text{pre}}_j - \sum_{k \neq j} w_{j,k} \, \tilde{Y}^{\text{pre}}_k \right\|^2\]

where \(\tilde{Y}^{\text{pre}}_j\) = demeaned pre-treatment outcome vector.

Debias all variables (outcome, treatment, instrument): \[\tilde{V}^{db}_{j,t} = V_{j,t} - \sum_k w_{j,k} \, V_{k,t}\]

for \(V \in \{\log(\text{Rent}),\; I^{pc},\; \text{Exposure},\; \text{Vote Share}, \ldots\}\)

SIV = 2SLS using SC-debiased versions of outcome, treatment, and instrument simultaneously.

Rent matching: 2004–2015 annual log rent (12 years, tight fit)

Election matching: 2006–2019 vote shares by party (5 elections \(\times\) 5 parties = 25 features)

  • Conservative SIV: regionally-blocked donor pools (ATL/QC/ON/PRA/BCTR)
  • Liberal SIV: unrestricted donors + \(\log(\text{income})\) control

Political Outcome Specifications

Reduced-form event study (political outcomes): \[(\text{VoteShare}^{db}_{j,t} - \text{VoteShare}^{db}_{j,2015}) = \delta_t \cdot \text{Exposure}^{db}_j + X'_j \pi + \varepsilon_{j,t}\]

Estimated separately for each election year \(t \in \{2006, 2008, 2011, 2019, 2021, 2025\}\), with 2015 as reference (\(\delta_{2015} = 0\)). Population-weighted, HC1 robust SEs.

2SLS long-difference (political outcomes):

Level: \[(\text{VoteShare}_{j,2025} - \text{VoteShare}_{j,2015}) \times 100 = \alpha + \beta \cdot \Delta I^{pc,L}_j + X'_j \pi + u_j\]

Differenced: \[(\text{VoteShare}_{j,2025} - \text{VoteShare}_{j,2021}) \times 100 = \alpha + \beta \cdot \Delta I^{pc,L}_j + X'_j \pi + u_j\]

\(\Delta I^{pc,L}_j\) instrumented with \(\text{Exposure}_j\). Coefficient \(\hat{\beta}\): pp change in vote share per 1 pp increase in cumulative immigration per capita.
